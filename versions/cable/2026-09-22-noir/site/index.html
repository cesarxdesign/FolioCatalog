v0.0Diagnosis

The product was the manual process, reproduced in software.

The team had built each step faithfully, and neglected the outcomes behind them, the results that mattered.

Asking people to change how they work, for a marginal gain, is a tough trade. The product had to create value, not mirror it.

The most important work was happening inside an attachment.

The account IDs under review arrived as a CSV uploaded into the chat, and the reviewing happened in there. The product was just how the file got delivered.

v1.0Hierarchy

I gave the one feature room, and the work a place to happen.

Two things were clear: the product had to grow, and it had to be worth using. The single screen setup blocked both. Paper Trails, the whole reason the product existed, carried the same visual weight as the logout button. The first step was to give it the space it deserved, with room to evolve. A high level view of all trails, each a card carrying its own state, a pattern users already knew. Then a dedicated view for the work that happened inside each one.

Every Paper Trail is a list of account IDs that someone has to review one by one. Those lines came out of the attachment and became a component of their own, sitting inside the trail, one click to copy each. Over 90% of users engaged with them, the most used component in the product. Same feature set. Different value proposition.

Fincrime tooling was new ground, so our pilot partners had little reference for what to ask for. Giving them something worth reacting to changed the quality of what came back.

v2.0Method

Designed for outcomes, not steps. The product blossomed.

I stopped asking users how they did the work, and started asking what outcome they needed, how they were getting there, and whether a better route existed. I kept the workshops frequent, and their insight gradually sharpened.

These were not technical people, so the start was slow. The first wins were small: supervisors spent their days assigning priority and owner, so I added both properties onto the cards. Trivial to us, a big deal to them.

The asks grew with the relationship. Soon small tweaks to existing elements became full features, used the moment they shipped:

Assurance

Every account and every transaction tested against the bank’s own controls, continuously. Compliance had been sampling a slice of it once a quarter.

Reports

Teams have to review the quality of their own decisions. The product drew the sample, asked the questions and returned the score, ready to put in front of a regulator.

Risk Assessment

The assessment every regulated firm has to produce each year, built in the product rather than a spreadsheet. Scored as you fill it in, locked once approved.

Actions

Every finding, wherever it surfaced, carried an owner and a date. Remediation happened where the finding was, instead of in somebody’s inbox.

The roadmap took shape as we went along. Without competitors or a blueprint to follow, I relied heavily on instinct and our pilot, and the UI began to strain under everything we added.

v3.0System

A bag of features, rebuilt as one system.

3.0 was design and engineering, end to end. By then I knew what the product had to be, so I could design all of it at once: find the patterns doing the same job in different ways, keep the strongest of each, and extend it to cover every case. Together we rebuilt the information architecture and the code beneath it.

It was a big commitment in resources, and a hard sell. But Oversight, our newest and most popular feature, was bursting at the seams more than any other, and with the banks’ interest in it rising, it pushed the redesign through.

Oversight, a flagship on par with Paper Trails

The sponsor banks wanted Cable inside Cable: the same product pointed at the fintechs they oversee, so they could read those controls the way they read their own. Working out how that could exist was mine, and one rule sat behind every decision, that a bank must never mistake a fintech’s Paper Trail for its own.

With the redesign committed, the two biggest changes were to the top and side navigation.

Top navigation

Oversight had stacked four headers on one screen. I folded them into a single bar that still told you which bank, which fintech and which feature you were in.

Side navigation

A reduced mode, icons only, giving the dense screens their width back without anyone losing their place.

The bag of features became a suite. Finished, solid, reliable, and the right amount of polish to be worth the price.

OutcomeAfter 3.0

Working isn’t enough. It has to be worth switching to.

“Compliance officers are conservative people.”

Natasha Vernier, co-founder and CEO of Cable
The SPL podcast, 2023

Banks buy slowly, on trust, and on a return that outweighs the disruption of changing how people work. Iteration by iteration, the product grew into one that cleared that. Tide, our pilot partner, converted first. Quaint Oak Bank, Griffin and Ramp followed, and in the years after, more than 50 banks and fintechs. Cable was acquired in 2026.

Design unlocked the value.
Value made it sell.
