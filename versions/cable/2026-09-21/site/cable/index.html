Overview

Banks have to prove their financial crime controls work. Cable built software to do it, and it worked. Nobody used it. There was one screen, a pilot client, Tide, using it for free, and not a single sale. I gave the flagship room, grew the product with its users, then rebuilt it into one suite banks paid for.

Pilot → Paid

Tide, the free pilot, became the first paying client. Quaint Oak Bank, Griffin and Ramp followed.

01
The problem

It worked. Nobody used it.

Cable did the job exactly the way users already did it by hand.

Why nobody switched

It automated the steps users already took by hand, and added very little on top.

Specs came straight from users describing their day, and each step they named became a UI element. It’s widely held that a tool has to be many times better, often put at ten, before people change how they work. Cable didn’t come close.

“The most important work in the product was happening inside an attachment.”

The account IDs users actually had to review lived in a database file, uploaded into a chat. The interface held everything except the work.

02
First move

First, give the flagship room to breathe.

v0.1 was so cramped that basic design principles were enough to start. I audited it and found the product hiding in it: Paper Trails, buried under everything built on top.

The audit

Four findings. Generous white space, two menu bars, content hidden inside the rows, flows triggered too far apart. Every new feature would have made it worse.

It was a first spark. And it got me talking to the users.

More views, less complexity

Cards, a metaphor users already knew from their project management tools. State on the card, Needs Reviewing, In Progress and Closed, let me drop the filter component and half the navigation.

Out of the attachment

The account IDs came out of the chat and into a list, one click to copy each. I wrote at the time: “This list would come to be the most important part of the whole product.”

03
Going to the source

Every feature came from a conversation.

Talking to the users, mostly MLROs running small fincrime teams, I stopped asking how they did their work and started asking what they needed to get done.

Built from behavior

Supervisors spent their time assigning Priority and Owner. So both moved onto the card.

Seen in behavior, then confirmed in interviews. Two pickers turned the card from something you open into something you work on.

No roadmap

Every feature ended with the same question: what else can we help with? The answer became the next feature.

Nobody planned ten features. They grew one interview at a time, until Cable was a suite a bank could actually buy.

The suite

Assurance, Reports, Comms & Docs, Risk Assessment, Typologies, Actions, Upload and Profile, with the Dashboard and onboarding that tie them together. The last three are Griffin, in its own brand, themed by hand from its guidelines.

04
Oversight

The banks asked for it. I had to work out how it could exist.

Sponsor banks wanted Cable on every fintech they oversee, and to see those fintechs’ controls the way they see their own. The second flagship: the same product, the same UI, different data. It had to show every partnership’s health at a glance, group them, and single one out to see what it sees.

Singled out

A bank must never mistake a fintech’s Paper Trail for its own.

Dashboard, Paper Trails and Assurance stay exactly as they are outside Oversight: familiar, and no second version of a feature to build.

The cues are visual. The fintech’s logo beside the secondary navigation and on every card, with the sidebar reinforcing the mode.

Past its limits

Every feature had its own way of doing things.

Patterns were added as needed, with no bigger picture. We figured it out as we went. Some features looked like they belonged to a different product, and Oversight stacked four headers on one screen.

05
Cable 3.0

One product, not ten.

3.0 wasn’t about new features. By now I knew what Cable was, not from a roadmap but from building it feature by feature. So I could look at every feature at once, and define one structure to serve them all.

A full engineering overhaul

The information architecture and the code underneath were rebuilt to carry the new patterns. The missing menu bar is only the visible part.

Unify, don’t add

Features that had stretched the old metaphors were brought into one system, so the whole suite reads as one cohesive product.

2.0

Four layers, stacked.

Each added when a feature needed it. It worked, but nothing held it together.

3.0

One bar, composed.

Views and context in a single floating bar. Clearer hierarchy, more room, one pattern for every feature.

90%+

The list from the attachment paid off. The ID lines became the most used component in the product.

06
Outcome

A product banks paid for.

With 3.0, Cable became a purchase worth making. When I left, one screen had become a suite of 10+ features, with two flagships carrying the sales.

Why it mattered

“Compliance officers are conservative people.”

They won’t put money into software that looks and feels early.

Natasha Vernier, co-founder and CEO · The SPL, 2023

Working isn’t enough. It has to be worth switching to.

How it’s going now

A few years after I left, Cable was acquired as a whole, by Synctera, in 2026. The deal came long after me; the road there was one I helped lay.

Cesar Garcia · Founding Designer · Cable
