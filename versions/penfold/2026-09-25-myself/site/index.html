Week 1Ship

Live in seven days. Fast, and ugly on purpose.

I knew nothing about pensions, and there was no mould to copy: even the old banks had no standard way of doing it online. I spent the first days learning everything the co-founders could teach me, then sketched my own version before opening their proof of concept, to keep the first attempt unbiased.

Their proof of concept had nearly all the right pieces. The job was to make it something the public could sign up to, in time for the funding round. I shipped the one shape the week allowed: a single flow, 33 steps long. Shorter would have taken longer than we had.

“If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”

After Blaise Pascal.

A dozen real people became customers in the first week, and the round closed on a product that was live and onboarding.

01Onboarding

People came back, and landed on step one.

The CTO ran the funnel daily and brought the drops to stand-up. I watched those sessions in FullStory, and session IDs showed what the funnel couldn’t: people who quit did come back. They just started from the beginning.

The account was the pension. Leave halfway, and you came back to nothing.

Nothing was saved until the end, because creating the account and creating the pension were the same event.

Using only pieces that already existed, so nothing in the code broke, I cut, regrouped and reordered the 33 steps:

Account first

I split the account from the pension. An email and a password save progress; the pension comes after, new or imported, whenever the user is ready.

Five modules

Five independent modules users can leave and resume, and the team can change one without touching the others.

One component set

Penfold was desktop only. Designing mobile, I found the components nearly identical, so I made mobile the single source feeding desktop. Built once instead of twice: half the engineering load, and we shipped daily.

The price: a few desktop screens fell below my own bar. Shipping daily mattered more than polish at that stage, and the team agreed. People who quit could now come back and finish where they left off.

02The click

It got better. A pension still took five days to exist.

Our partners, Gaudi and Seccl, worked by hand. Each step waited up to 24, 48 or 72 hours on an ID from the one before, and legally the pension existed only once the last one came back. Users finished onboarding and were told to hold on while we ran some checks. For days.

My first try was language: call it a pot until the pension existed. It broke on the first top-up, because the money had nowhere legal to go. The partners couldn’t be sped up either.

What I noticed

The IDs always came back, with zero failures, and engineering confirmed a wrong reference would be flagged and replaced on the same record. So we stopped waiting: provisional references, with the user’s real details, went to every partner the moment the information module was done, and a ledger swapped each one for the real reference as it arrived.

A pension now existed the moment onboarding ended, every reference reconciled within the week, zero failures. I came to design from software engineering, and this is what that background is for: designing in the material, not around it.

And yet

Onboarding worked. Pensions were instant. Still, 100 signups a month.

So we went looking, closer to the people using it.

03Listening

Customer Support was the gold mine.

I built a loop around the people already using Penfold:

Every morning

Session replays

I started each day in FullStory, noting the users worth talking to, and reached out when a session raised a question only they could answer.

Every Friday

Top tickets

Customer Support and I went through the week’s top tickets: complaints, requests and feedback, straight from the source.

Every week

A beta user in the room

I started a beta programme, recruited from those sessions and tickets, and invited one beta user to join the Friday chat.

Every fix

A message back

We logged who had raised what. When it was fixed, I messaged them myself: that thing that bugged you, we fixed it, thanks for telling us.

Watching someone go from annoyed to genuinely pleased, because a person had actually listened.

The thing I’m proudest of building at Penfold. It gave the company a human feel, and it shaped how the whole team worked, not just design.

“What if I die?”

Joe T. asked it as a joke on a Friday chat. None of my flows had ever considered death; behind the scenes it was an email carrying four data points. I sketched one screen, three inputs plus the name we already had, and wrote a one-line spec. It was Tech-Debt-Friday, every component already existed, and it shipped that afternoon, in time for me to call Joe back: everyone can now nominate a beneficiary.

Trust went up, and Customer Support stopped doing nominations by hand. Joe T. was happy. We weren’t. Signups hadn’t moved.

04The flip

We were selling to the wrong people. So I changed who it was for.

Penfold sold pensions to people who didn’t have one. A huge market, and a slow one: they didn’t feel they needed a pension, so every signup had to be educated first.

In a meeting, I argued for the opposite: sell to people who already have pensions, scattered across past employers. They needed no convincing. They needed a better pension, and that’s a product problem.

The battleground moved from educating people to winning them on product. And we were fast at product.

The message changed, then the Facebook targeting and spend, over November and December 2019. I redid the website and the ads myself, outside my discipline: October’s “You may already be losing up to £228.95 daily” became “Find and combine” by 9 December.

Importing a pension had been one of three main actions on the dashboard since the MVP. I made Combine the flagship, and the priority:

Bad versions still count

I hated the first two versions of the form, because I knew they weren’t the right experience. I shipped them anyway: each beat what we had, and the first one literally beat nothing. Together they carried hundreds of Combine requests.

Then doing it right

The form asked for the provider’s address, which nobody knows. We compiled every provider’s address ourselves, dropped the question, and swapped free text for a dropdown that always matches.

Complexity, added on purpose

I usually take things away. Here I added a field the API didn’t need, the employer each pot came from, so people combining several pots could tell their requests apart.

Transaction history, designed backwards

It waited its turn for months: without the features that create transactions, there was nothing to show. Then I designed it as far into the future as I could, and dialled it back to an MVP of three properties. Each version after shipped on its own, like Lego, until a combined pot landed in the list with a notification.

OutcomeYear one

Signups went from 100 to 600 a month, each arriving with a pot.

Signup value rose with volume, since every new user brought a pension to combine, and acquisition costs fell.

A dozen users in week one became around 3,000 savers and £4M under management by the end of year one, on plan. Trustpilot and Net Promoter Score both scored at the top, with hundreds of independent reviews.
