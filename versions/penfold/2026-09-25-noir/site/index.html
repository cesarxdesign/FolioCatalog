Penfold · Pensions · B2C fintech, mobile and web

£4M in year one.
Onboarding and three features.

Penfold’s co-founders pitched it as “We want to make pensions sexy.” My answer: “Impossible. I’m in.” I was their first hire. This is how a week-old product became 3,000 savers and £4M under management in twelve months.

Week 1Ship

Seven days to go live. So I did.

When I joined, the co-founders had a proof of concept and a funding round coming. I had seven days to turn one into a live product for the other, knowing nothing about pensions.

If you can’t make onboarding quick, make it manageable. That week, we couldn’t make it either. It shipped as one flow, 33 steps long, because shorter would have taken longer than we had.

“If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”

After Blaise Pascal. A dozen real people became customers that week, and the round closed on a product that was live and onboarding.

01One set

Then straight back in. Mobile, and finish later.

As soon as we delivered, I went back in for what users already wanted: mobile, and an onboarding you could finish later. Penfold was desktop only. Mobile would double the engineering, and we couldn’t afford twice the developers.

So I designed one component set that fed both. Built once, shipped on both.

Mobile became the single source for desktop. Not the norm then. Half the engineering load, and we shipped daily.

The price: a few desktop screens fell below my own bar. Shipping daily mattered more.

02The snag

Finish later hit a snag. The account was the pension.

The CTO ran the funnel daily. In FullStory, session IDs showed what the funnel couldn’t: people who quit did come back. They just landed on step one.

No pension meant no account. No account meant nothing saved.

Creating the account and creating the pension were the same event, so an unfinished onboarding left nothing to come back to.

An empty pension

So we made one. An email and a password create the account and an empty pension behind it, progress saves, and the rest is filled in whenever the user is ready.

Five modules

The 33 steps, regrouped into five modules users can leave and resume, from pieces that already existed, so nothing in the code broke.

People who quit could now come back and finish. Easy enough. But there was a bigger problem.

03The click

A pension took a week to exist. We made it instant.

Three partners had to issue an ID, in order, before a pension legally existed, each one waiting on the one before. Users finished onboarding and were told to hold on while we ran some checks. For days.

Days

By hand. Tolerable.

Days more

Gaudi

By hand, after Seccl. The one we dreaded.

Seconds

HSBC

Automated, after Gaudi. Holds the money.

My first try was language: call it a pot until the pension existed. It broke on the first top-up, because the money had nowhere legal to go. The partners couldn’t be sped up either.

So we advanced the IDs

The IDs always came back, and a wrong reference would be flagged and replaced on the same record. So we stopped waiting: provisional references went to every partner at once, and a ledger swapped each for the real ID as it arrived.

A week became a cuppa tea’s worth. And the user never saw the wait.

The pension appeared the moment onboarding ended, and the paperwork caught up behind it. Zero failures. I came to design from software engineering, and this is what that background is for: designing in the material, not around it.

And yet

An instant pension. And very few wanted it. 100 signups a month.

So I looked into every nook and cranny for what the product was missing.

“What if I die?”

Joe T. asked it as a joke on a Friday chat. None of my flows had considered death. One screen, a one-line spec, existing components: it shipped that afternoon, in time for me to call Joe back.

Joe was happy. We weren’t. Signups hadn’t moved.

The loop is what I’m proudest of building at Penfold. It gave the company a human feel. It didn’t fix growth.

05The flip

We were selling to the wrong people. So I changed who it was for.

From the start we wanted to educate people without a pension. A huge market, and a slow one. To educate, we needed critical mass, and that we had to take rather than gain.

I argued for the opposite: go for people who already had pensions, scattered across past employers, and beat their providers on product. They needed no convincing. And we were fast at product.

The battleground moved from educating people to winning them on product.

Message, targeting and spend changed by December 2019. I redid the website and ads myself, outside my discipline: “You may already be losing up to £228.95 daily” became “Find and combine”.

Importing a pension had been on the dashboard since the MVP. I made Combine the flagship:

Bad versions still count

I hated the first two versions of the form, and shipped them anyway: each beat what we had. They carried hundreds of requests.

Then doing it right

The form asked for the provider’s address, which nobody knows. We compiled them all ourselves and dropped the question.

Complexity, on purpose

I usually take things away. Here I added a field the API didn’t need, the employer, so several pots could be told apart.

OutcomeYear one

That’s what cracked it. 100 signups a month became 600.

We had already built the better experience, so we were ready for the influx. Everything we made chasing the harder market paid off on the flood from the competition. Every new user arrived with a pot, so signup value rose with volume and acquisition costs fell.

A dozen users in week one became around 3,000 savers and £4M under management by the end of year one, on plan, with Trustpilot and Net Promoter Score at the top.

Ship it ugly.
Make it instant.
Sell it to the right people.

The foundation held. Penfold now manages over £1B and was voted Best Pension 2026.
