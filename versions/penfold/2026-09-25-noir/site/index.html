Ours took minutes.

First hire, lured by an impossible, and irresistible mandate to “make pensions sexy”. Shipped an ugly, slow, and cumbersome first version within 7 days. And in less than a year, iterated our way into 3,000 users and £4M under management.

Week 1Ship

If I had more time, I’d’ve made a shorter onboarding.

I joined Penfold 7 days before a funding deadline, and the round needed a live product with real customers. I knew nothing about pensions. So I sketched while reading the code of our proof of concept, turning its main functions into onboarding screens.

Our first flow was 33 steps long, took more than 10 minutes to finish, and needed about 5 days to set up a pension.

Funding secured.

01Onboarding

Onboarding done.
Next: onboarding.

People dropped off along the 33 steps. Session recordings showed what the numbers couldn’t: those who quit did come back later, only to start again from step one.

Nothing was saved until step 33. The account and the pension were created together, at the very end.

So I moved them to the start. The account, with an empty pension inside it, now opened first, and everything people entered was saved to it. The 33 steps became five modules they could leave and pick up later. If you can’t make it quick, make it manageable.

While rebuilding the flow, I added mobile. Penfold was desktop only, and a second platform would double the work of our one developer. So I designed one component set for both, and every screen was built once. We shipped daily.

02The wait

Sign up today. Get your pension next week.

Finishing sign-up didn’t give you a pension. Penfold didn’t open pensions itself: three partner companies did, one after another, each waiting on an ID from the one before. Until all three were done, the pension didn’t legally exist, and nobody could pay in. Users were told to hold on while we ran some checks. For a week.

Automated. Holds the money.

My first fix was a word: call it a “pot” until the pension existed. It broke the first time someone topped up. There was still no pension to put the money in.

The IDs always came back.

Not one had ever failed, and a wrong ID could be corrected later on the same record. So we stopped waiting in line. As soon as you’d entered your details, all three partners got a provisional ID at once, and a ledger swapped in each real one as it arrived.

A pension now opened a couple of minutes after sign-up: a cuppa’s worth, down from a week. This is where my software engineering background paid off. The fix was in the plumbing, not the screens.

03Listening

A pension in minutes. Still 100 signups a month.

Signing up was manageable and the pension was fast, yet only about 100 new people a month were joining. So I got closer to the ones who had. Every Friday, Customer Support and I went through the week’s top complaints and requests, with one of our users in the room.

“What if I die?”

A user, Joe T., asked it as a joke one Friday. Penfold had no answer on screen: naming who inherits your pension was a manual email behind the scenes. I sketched one screen and wrote a one-line spec. Every part of it already existed, so it shipped that afternoon, in time for me to call Joe back.

Joe was happy. We weren’t: signups hadn’t moved.

04The flip

We were selling to the wrong people. So I changed who it was for.

Penfold sold pensions to people who didn’t have one yet, mostly the self-employed. Each had to be convinced they needed a pension before they would sign up. A huge market, and a slow one.

I argued for the opposite: people who already had pensions, left behind with old employers. They didn’t need convincing. They needed a better pension, and we were fast at product.

The feature they needed had been on the dashboard since launch.

Combine: move your old pensions into Penfold. I made it the flagship, and rewrote the website and the ads around it myself. “You may already be losing up to £228.95 daily” became “Find and combine.”

Bad versions still count

I hated the first two versions of the Combine form. I shipped them anyway: each was better than what we had, and together they carried hundreds of transfer requests.

Then doing it right

The form asked for your old provider’s postal address. Nobody knows that. We compiled every provider’s address ourselves, and the question disappeared.

OutcomeYear one

Six times the signups. Each one brought a pension.

People with old pensions came over from other providers, and signups went from 100 to 600 a month. Everything built while nobody was coming, the fast pension, the onboarding you could finish later, the Friday sessions, was ready for them. Each new user arrived with a pension to transfer, so every signup was worth more, and acquisition costs fell.
