Pensions took days.
Ours took minutes.

Seven days to go live, 33 steps to sign up, a week to get a pension, 100 signups a month. Once the wait went from a week to minutes, and we sold to people who already had a pension, signups rose six times and year one closed at £4M under management.

Week 1Ship

Seven days to go live. Knowing nothing about pensions.

I joined as the first hire. The co-founders had a proof of concept and a funding round, and the round needed a live product in a week. If you can’t make onboarding quick, make it manageable at least. We couldn’t make it quick. It shipped as one flow, 33 steps long.

“If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”

After Blaise Pascal.

A dozen people became customers that week, and the round closed.

01Onboarding

People came back. To step one.

In FullStory, session IDs showed what the funnel couldn’t: people who quit did come back. And started again from the beginning. Creating the account and creating the pension were one event, so nothing was saved until the very end.

The account was the pension. Quit halfway, and there was nothing to come back to.

So we made an empty pension. It’s created with the account and holds your progress, and the 33 steps became five modules you can leave and pick up again. While I was in there, I made mobile the single source for desktop: one component set, half the engineering, and we shipped daily. Not the norm then.

02The wait

A pension took a week. We told people to hold on.

Three partners had to issue an ID, in order, each waiting on the one before. Only then did the pension legally exist. Users finished onboarding and were told to hold on while we ran some checks. For days.

My first try was words: call it a pot until the pension existed. It broke on the first top-up. The money had nowhere legal to go.

The IDs always came back.

Not one had ever failed, and a wrong reference could be replaced on the same record. So we stopped waiting. Provisional references went to all three partners at once, and a ledger swapped in each real one as it arrived.

A week became a couple of minutes, a cuppa tea’s worth. I came to design from software engineering. This is what that’s for: designing in the material, not around it.

03Listening

A pension in minutes. Still 100 signups a month.

The product worked. Nobody new was coming. So we got closer to the people who had: every Friday, Customer Support and I went through the week’s top tickets, with one beta user in the room. Support was the gold mine.

“What if I die?”

Joe T. asked it as a joke. None of my flows had ever considered death. I sketched one screen and wrote a one-line spec; every component already existed. It shipped that afternoon, in time for me to call Joe back.

Joe was happy. We weren’t. Signups hadn’t moved.

04The flip

We were selling to the wrong people. So I changed who it was for.

Penfold sold pensions to people who didn’t have one. Before they could sign up, they had to be convinced they needed one. A huge market, and a slow one. I argued for the opposite: people who already had pensions, scattered across old employers. They needed no convincing, just a better pension. That’s a product fight, and we were fast at product.

What they needed had been on the dashboard since the MVP.

Combine: bring your old pensions into one. I made it the flagship, and redid the website and the ads myself. “You may already be losing up to £228.95 daily” became “Find and combine.”

Bad versions still count

I hated the first two versions of the Combine form. I shipped them anyway: each beat what we had, and together they carried hundreds of requests.

Then doing it right

The form asked for your old provider’s address. Nobody knows that. We compiled every provider’s address ourselves and dropped the question.

OutcomeYear one

The flood came. We were ready for it.

Everything built while nobody was coming, the pension in minutes, the onboarding you could finish later, the Friday loop, met the people coming over from the competition. Signups went from 100 to 600 a month, each arriving with a pension to combine. Signup value rose with volume, and acquisition costs fell.
