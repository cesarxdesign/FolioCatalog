Wrong people.
Right product.

6xSignups a month

We were selling to the wrong people. So I changed who it was for.

Context

By late 2019 onboarding worked, pensions opened instantly, and we were listening to users every week. The product did the job. We still had 100 signups a month.

Penfold sold pensions to people who didn’t have one. A huge market, and a slow one: people without a pension didn’t feel they needed one, so every signup had to be educated first.

In a meeting, I argued for the opposite: sell to people who already have pensions, the pots scattered across every past employer. They needed no convincing that pensions matter. They just needed a better one.

The battleground moved from educating people to winning them on product. And we were fast at product.

We changed the message, then the Facebook targeting and spend, over November and December 2019. In October the site led with “The bad news: you may already be losing up to £228.95 daily.” By 9 December it led with “Find and combine.”

Signups went from about 100 to about 600 a month, and every one arrived with a pension to combine, so signup value rose with volume while acquisition costs fell. A dozen users in the first week became around 3,000 savers and £4M under management by the end of year one, on plan.

The product was ready for the switch, because importing a pension had been one of three main actions on the dashboard since the MVP. It just hadn’t been the point. Combine became the flagship, and I made it the priority:

Combine, iterated in public

I hated the first two versions of the form, because I knew they weren’t the right experience. But each beat what we had, the first one literally beat nothing, and together they carried hundreds of Combine requests. Doing it right came later: we compiled every provider’s address ourselves, stopped asking users for one nobody knows, and swapped a free text field for a dropdown.

Complexity, added on purpose

I usually take things away. Here the better experience needed one more field: the employer each pot came from. The API didn’t require it, but it let people keep track of several Combine requests at once.

Transaction history, designed backwards

It was missing for the longest time: without the features that create transactions, there would be none to show. When its turn came, I designed it as far into the future as I could, then dialled it back to an MVP with three properties.

Built up like Lego

Each iteration shipped on its own and set up the next: types before filters, then totals, then notifications for Combine events, until a combined pot landed in the list. Engineering always had room for other priorities in between.

7 daysConcept to live

Live in a week, in time for the funding round.

I knew nothing about pensions, and there was no mould to copy: even the old banks had no standard way of doing it online. I spent the first days learning everything the co-founders could teach me, and sketched my own version before looking at their proof of concept, to keep the first attempt unbiased.

Their proof of concept had nearly all the right pieces. The job was to make it something the public could sign up to, from an email address to owning a pension. The week allowed one shape: a single linear flow, 33 steps long. Fast, and ugly on purpose.

“If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”

After Blaise Pascal. 33 steps was the length the week could afford.

A dozen real people became customers in the first week, and the round closed on a product that was live and onboarding.

InstantDown from 5 days

A pension took five days to exist. I made it instant.

Our partners, Gaudi and Seccl, worked by hand. Each step waited up to 24, 48 or 72 hours on an ID from the step before it, and legally the pension only existed once the last one came back. Users finished onboarding and were told to hold on while we ran some checks. For days.

My first try was language: call it a pot until the pension existed. That broke on the first top-up, because the money had nowhere legal to go. The partners couldn’t be sped up either.

What I noticed

The IDs always came back, with zero failures, and engineering confirmed a wrong reference would be flagged and replaced on the same record. So we stopped waiting for them. As soon as the information module was done, we sent provisional references, with the user’s real details, to every partner at once, and a ledger swapped each one for the real reference as it arrived.

A pension now existed the moment onboarding ended, and every reference was reconciled within the week, with zero failures. I came to design from software engineering, and this is what that background is for: designing in the material, rather than around it.

½The engineering load

One component set, five modules, shipping daily.

Context

The week-one launch was a single 33-step flow, desktop only. It had closed the round. Now it had to grow.

The CTO ran the funnel every day and brought the drops to stand-up. I watched those sessions in FullStory, and session IDs showed what the funnel couldn’t: people who quit did come back. They just started from the beginning.

The account was the pension. Leave halfway, and you came back to nothing.

Nothing was saved until the very end, because creating the account and creating the pension were the same event.

Account first

An email and a password opened an account and saved progress. The pension came after, created new or imported, whenever the user was ready.

Five modules

Using only pieces that already existed, so nothing broke, the 33 steps became five modules users could leave and resume, and the team could change one without touching the others.

One component set

Designing mobile, I found desktop and mobile components nearly identical, so mobile became the single source feeding desktop. Built once instead of twice.

Engineering load halved, and we shipped daily. It was a trade I made knowingly: a few desktop screens ended up below my own bar, because shipping mattered more than polish at that stage, and the team agreed.

1 dayJoke to production

The loop I’m proudest of. Customer Support was the gold mine.

Every morning

Session replays

I started each day in FullStory, noting the users worth talking to, and reached out when a session raised a question only they could answer.

Every Friday

Top tickets

Customer Support and I went through the week’s top tickets together: the pulse on complaints, requests and feedback, straight from the source.

Every week

A beta user in the room

I started a beta programme, recruited from those sessions and tickets, and invited one beta user to join the Friday chat, in person.

Every fix

A message back

We logged who had raised what. When it was fixed, I messaged them myself: that thing that bugged you, we fixed it, thanks for telling us.

Watching someone go from annoyed to genuinely pleased, because a person had actually listened.

It gave the company a human feel, users talked about it on social media, and it shaped how the whole team worked, not just design.

“What if I die?”

Joe T. asked it as a joke on a Friday chat. None of my flows had ever considered death. Behind the scenes it was an email to a third party carrying four data points. So I sketched one screen, three inputs plus the name we already had, and wrote a one-line spec. It was Tech-Debt-Friday, the developers wanted an excuse to dodge it, and every component already existed. It shipped that afternoon, in time for me to call Joe back: thanks to your suggestion, everyone can now nominate a beneficiary.

Trust went up, Customer Support stopped doing nominations by hand, and the speed said more about the system and the team than about the screen.

Right product.
Then, the right people.

The foundation held. Penfold now manages over £1B and was voted Best Pension 2026.
