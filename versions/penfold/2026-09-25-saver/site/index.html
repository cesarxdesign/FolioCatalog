Week 1Before any saver

First, there had to be something to sign up to. It took a week.

I knew nothing about pensions, and there was no mould to copy: even the old banks had no standard way of doing it online. I spent the first days learning everything the co-founders could teach me, sketched my own version before looking at their proof of concept, then turned that proof of concept into a product the public could use, from an email address to owning a pension. The week allowed one shape: a single flow, 33 steps long. Fast, and ugly on purpose.

“If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”

After Blaise Pascal. A dozen real customers signed up in the first week, and the funding round closed on a product that was live and onboarding. Everything below is what happened to the people who came next.

01Finds us

The saver already had a pension. Several, in fact.

At first, they wouldn’t have found us, because we weren’t looking for them. Penfold sold pensions to people without one: a huge market, and a slow one. People who don’t have a pension don’t feel they need one, so every signup had to be educated first. We sat at 100 signups a month.

In a meeting, I argued for the opposite: sell to people who already have pensions, pots scattered across every past employer. They need no convincing that pensions matter. They just need a better one, and that’s a product problem. We were fast at product.

Signups went from 100 to 600 a month, and every one arrived with a pension to bring.

We changed the message, then the Facebook targeting and spend, over November and December 2019. Signup value rose with volume, and acquisition costs fell.

I did a quick pass on the website and the ads too, outside my discipline. In October the site told them “The bad news: you may already be losing up to £228.95 daily.” By 9 December it told them to “Find and combine.”

02Signs up

They can leave halfway, and come back to where they were.

The week-one product lost people at every one of its 33 steps. The CTO ran the funnel every day and brought the drops to stand-up. I watched those sessions in FullStory, and session IDs showed what the funnel couldn’t: people who quit did come back. They just started again from step one.

The account was the pension. Leave halfway, and you came back to nothing.

Nothing was saved until the very end, because creating the account and creating the pension were the same event.

Account first

An email and a password open an account and save progress. The pension comes after, new or imported, whenever the saver is ready.

Five modules

Using only pieces that already existed, so nothing broke, the 33 steps became five modules the saver can leave and resume, and the team can change one without touching the rest.

On their phone

Penfold was desktop only. Designing mobile, I found the components nearly identical, so mobile became the single source feeding desktop. Built once instead of twice: half the engineering load, and we shipped daily.

When the saver comes back, a progress card shows exactly where they left off. The price was a few desktop screens below my own bar, a trade I made knowingly, because shipping mattered more than polish at that stage.

03Owns a pension

The pension exists the moment they finish.

It used to take five days. Our partners, Gaudi and Seccl, worked by hand. Each step waited up to 24, 48 or 72 hours on an ID from the step before it, and legally the pension only existed once the last one came back. The saver finished onboarding and was told to hold on while we ran some checks.

My first try was language: call it a pot until the pension existed. That broke on the saver’s first top-up, because the money had nowhere legal to go. The partners couldn’t be sped up either.

What I noticed

The IDs always came back, with zero failures, and engineering confirmed a wrong reference would be flagged and replaced on the same record. So we stopped waiting for them. As soon as the saver finished the information module, we sent provisional references, with their real details, to every partner at once, and a ledger swapped each one for the real reference as it arrived.

Every reference was reconciled within the week, with zero failures, and the saver never saw any of it. I came to design from software engineering, and this is what that background is for: designing in the material, rather than around it.

04Brings old pots

Every old pension, into one.

This is what the saver came for. Importing a pension had been one of three main actions on the dashboard since the MVP, with “Find my other pensions” added from the beta. Once we sold to people with pots, Combine became the flagship, and I made it the priority.

Bad versions still count

I hated the first two versions of the form, because I knew they weren’t the right experience. But each beat what we had, the first one literally beat nothing, and together they carried hundreds of Combine requests.

Then doing it right

The form asked for the provider’s address, which no saver ever knows. We compiled every provider’s address ourselves, dropped the question, and swapped a free text field for a dropdown that always matches.

Complexity, added on purpose

I usually take things away. Here the better experience needed one more field, the employer each pot came from. The API didn’t need it, but savers combining several pots did, to tell their requests apart.

Weighed every time

Every iteration was compared with what we had and what it would cost. I’m good at estimating engineering effort, from my years as a developer, and I always checked with the developers anyway.

05Watches it land

The old pot arrives, and they can see it happen.

For the longest time the saver had no transaction history at all. It was a glaring gap, but without the features that create transactions there would be nothing to show, so those came first.

When its turn came, I designed it backwards: as far into the future as I could, then dialled it back to an MVP, a plain list with three properties. Every version after it shipped on its own and set up the next, like Lego: types before filters, then yearly and lifetime views and totals, then notifications for Combine events.

The last piece: a combined pot landing in the list, with a notification to say so.

Leaving the most complex, least essential parts for last gave engineering room for other priorities between versions, without the saver ever getting a half-built screen.

06Asks the hard question

“What if I die?” Answered by the afternoon.

One saver, Joe T., asked it as a joke, on a Friday chat with our team. He was there because of a loop I built around the people using Penfold:

Every morning

Session replays

I started each day in FullStory, noting the savers worth talking to, and reached out when a session raised a question only they could answer.

Every Friday

Top tickets

Customer Support and I went through the week’s top tickets together. Support was the gold mine: complaints, requests and feedback, straight from the source.

Every week

A saver in the room

I started a beta programme, recruited from those sessions and tickets, and invited one beta saver to join the Friday chat, in person.

Every fix

A message back

We logged who had raised what. When it was fixed, I messaged them myself: that thing that bugged you, we fixed it, thanks for telling us.

Joe’s question

None of my flows had ever considered death. Behind the scenes it was an email to a third party carrying four data points. So I sketched one screen, three inputs plus the name we already had, and wrote a one-line spec. It was Tech-Debt-Friday, the developers wanted an excuse to dodge it, and every component already existed. It shipped that afternoon, in time for me to call Joe back: thanks to your suggestion, everyone can now nominate a beneficiary.

Watching someone go from annoyed to genuinely pleased, because a person had actually listened.

That loop is what I’m proudest of building at Penfold. It gave the company a human feel, savers talked about it on social media, and it shaped how the whole team worked, not just design.

12 savers in week one.
3,000 by the end of year one.

With £4M under management, on plan. The foundation held: Penfold now manages over £1B and was voted Best Pension 2026.
